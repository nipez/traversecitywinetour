Traverse City Wines for Thanksgiving: What to Pour from Old Mission and Leelanau

~10 min read  ·  November 2026  ·  Traverse City Wine Country Journal
Home› Journal› Traverse City Wines for Thanksgiving

Thanksgiving dinner is a mash-up of sweet, salty, herbal, and rich — turkey, cranberry, stuffing, gravy, roasted roots, and whatever else lands on the table. That mix is exactly where northern Michigan wine shines. Cool-climate bottles from Old Mission and Leelanau bring bright acidity, flexible sweetness levels, and food-friendly reds that do not steamroll the bird.

If you are hosting in Michigan — or visiting before Thanksgiving Day on November 26, 2026 — this guide covers which styles to pour, which local tasting rooms are known for them, and how to pick bottles up on a November tasting day. For a broader cold-season route, pair this with our winter wineries guide.

November hours are not summer hours. Confirm each tasting room the day before (or morning of) before you drive out for a holiday bottle run.

Why Traverse City wines fit the Thanksgiving table

Old Mission and Leelanau sit on the same latitude band as classic cool-climate regions, and the lake effect keeps acidity lively in the bottle. That acidity is the secret weapon with Thanksgiving food: it cuts through gravy, refreshes the palate after stuffing, and stands up to cranberry without tasting thin. You do not need one “perfect” wine — you need a short flight of styles that cover the meal from appetizers through pie.

A practical holiday spread usually includes one sparkling for the start, one white (or two at different sweetness levels), one lighter red, and optionally a dessert pour. Everything below uses wineries already on our trail maps; browse the full list on All Wineries or compare peninsulas in Plan Your Visit.

Dry and semi-dry Riesling

Riesling is the Thanksgiving workhorse. Dry and off-dry versions handle turkey, herbs, and cranberry better than most Chardonnay. Semi-dry bottles soften spicy sides and glazed vegetables without tasting dessert-sweet.

On Old Mission, start with Chateau Grand Traverse and Left Foot Charley. On Leelanau, Shady Lane Cellars, Good Harbor Vineyards, and Gill's Pier are reliable Riesling stops. For a deeper dive into styles and sweetness levels, see our best Rieslings guide.

Sparkling wine for the first pour

Open the meal with bubbles. Sparkling resets the palate, pairs with salty starters, and feels celebratory without demanding attention through the whole dinner. Michigan méthode traditionnelle and pét-nat styles both work; keep a second bottle chilled if guests linger over appetizers.

L. Mawby on Leelanau is the region's sparkling specialist — our L. Mawby sparkling guide covers why. On Old Mission, Chateau Chantal pours sparkling options worth tasting on a November stop, and Left Foot Charley often has lively pétillant naturel for a less formal toast.

Pinot Noir for turkey and mushroom sides

When the table wants red, cool-climate Pinot Noir is the safest bet with roast turkey, mushroom stuffing, and cranberry. Look for bright fruit and soft tannin — not a heavy, oaky bottle that fights the bird.

Strong local bets include Shady Lane and Gill's Pier on Leelanau, plus Bel Lago, 2 Lads Winery, and Bonobo Winery. Our Pinot Noir tasting guide maps a fuller red-wine route if you want more options before the holiday.

Gewürztraminer and Pinot Blanc

Aromatic whites earn a seat when the menu leans sweet-savory: roasted squash, spiced nuts, sausage stuffing, or a touch of heat. Gewürztraminer brings spice and florals; Pinot Blanc stays cleaner and food-flexible.

For Gewürztraminer, look to Peninsula Cellars on Old Mission and Bel Lago or Ciccone Vineyard on Leelanau. Pinot Blanc fans should taste at Brys Estate, 2 Lads, and Forty-Five North.

Cabernet Franc when the red drinkers want more structure

Cabernet Franc is northern Michigan's answer to “is there a bigger red for Thanksgiving?” It usually shows herbal notes and firmer structure than Pinot without the weight of a hot-climate Cabernet Sauvignon — useful next to dark-meat turkey, ham, or a vegetarian main with roasted mushrooms.

Seek it at 2 Lads and Forty-Five North, plus Shady Lane, Ciccone, and Black Star Farms. Pour it slightly cooler than room temperature so the herbal edge stays fresh.

Dessert and ice wine for the pie course

Save something sweet for pecan, pumpkin, or apple pie. Late-harvest Riesling and ice wine are Michigan specialties; a small pour goes a long way. You can also open dessert wine earlier with a cheese board if the pie course is crowded.

Chateau Grand Traverse and Black Star Farms are classic ice-wine and late-harvest destinations. Mari Vineyards (Bestow) and Ciccone (Dolce Vita) offer dessert styles worth tasting when you are already on the trail. Keep these bottles well chilled.

A simple Thanksgiving bottle plan

For eight adults: two sparkling, two Riesling (one drier, one semi-dry), two Pinot Noir or Cabernet Franc, and one dessert bottle. That covers a long meal without running dry or overbuying. Scale up for larger groups; scale down if several guests do not drink.

How much to buy, how cold to serve

A rough rule for a multi-hour holiday meal is about half a bottle per adult who drinks wine, then add a buffer bottle of the most popular style. For mixed groups, bias toward white and sparkling — they disappear first with appetizers and turkey.

  • Sparkling: well chilled, roughly refrigerator-cold
  • Riesling, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Blanc: chilled, then warm slightly in the glass
  • Pinot Noir and Cabernet Franc: cool cellar temp — not warm kitchen counter
  • Dessert and ice wine: cold; small pours

If you are tasting and buying the same day, ask staff to keep purchased bottles in a cooler bag or leave them in a cool car trunk between stops. Do not leave whites on a sunny dash while you finish the route.

Picking up bottles on a November tasting day

Mid-to-late November is shoulder season on both peninsulas. Many tasting rooms stay open, but schedules often shrink to weekends or shorter daily windows — the same pattern we cover in the winter guide. Build a short list, confirm hours, then taste with purchase intent.

  1. Choose one peninsula for a half-day bottle run, or both only if you start early and have a confirmed list.
  2. Shortlist three to five wineries that cover your styles — for example L. Mawby (sparkling), Shady Lane (Riesling and Pinot), and Black Star Farms (dessert).
  3. Call or check each winery's site for current November hours before you leave downtown.
  4. Taste first, buy second — ask which current releases are driest or most food-friendly for turkey.
  5. If the whole household is tasting, book a wine tour or browse wine tour guides so nobody has to stay sober behind the wheel with a trunk full of holiday bottles.

Old Mission is usually the tighter loop if you are short on time; Leelanau rewards a fuller day with more space between estates. Either way, treat hours as variable — off-season schedules change, and holiday weeks can shift further.
